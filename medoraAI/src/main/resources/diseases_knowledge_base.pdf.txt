# Infection
An infection is a disease caused by the invasion and multiplication of microorganisms such as bacteria, viruses, fungi, or parasites in the body. These pathogens can enter the body through the skin, respiratory tract, digestive system, or bloodstream. The body's immune system responds by producing white blood cells, antibodies, and inflammation to fight the invaders. Common signs of infection include fever, swelling, redness, pain, fatigue, and increased white blood cell count. Types of infections: Bacterial infections (e.g., strep throat, UTI, pneumonia) are treated with antibiotics. Viral infections (e.g., flu, common cold, HIV) are treated with antiviral medications or supportive care. Fungal infections (e.g., athlete's foot, yeast infections) are treated with antifungal medications. Parasitic infections (e.g., malaria, giardia) are treated with antiparasitic drugs. Localized infections affect one area of the body, while systemic infections spread throughout the body and can be life-threatening (sepsis). Prevention includes vaccination, handwashing, safe food handling, safe sex practices, and avoiding contact with sick individuals. When to see a doctor: high fever (above 103°F/39.4°C), persistent symptoms lasting more than a week, difficulty breathing, severe pain, confusion, or signs of sepsis (rapid heart rate, rapid breathing, extreme cold or sweating).

# Diabetes
Diabetes is a chronic condition affecting how the body processes blood sugar. Type 1 diabetes is an autoimmune disease where the pancreas produces little or no insulin. Type 2 diabetes develops when the body becomes resistant to insulin and cannot keep blood sugar at normal levels. Symptoms include frequent urination, excessive thirst, increased hunger, fatigue, blurred vision, slow-healing sores, and tingling in hands or feet. Diagnosis is confirmed by fasting blood glucose (normal: 70-100 mg/dL, prediabetes: 100-125 mg/dL, diabetes: 126 mg/dL or higher), HbA1c (normal: below 5.7%, prediabetes: 5.7-6.4%, diabetes: 6.5% or higher), or random glucose (diabetes: 200 mg/dL or higher with symptoms). Treatment includes diet modification, regular exercise, weight management, oral medications like metformin, and insulin therapy. Complications include kidney disease, nerve damage, eye disease, heart disease, and stroke.

# Hypertension
Hypertension (high blood pressure) is often called the silent killer because it usually has no symptoms. Normal blood pressure is below 120/80 mmHg. Elevated is 120-129/<80. Stage 1 hypertension is 130-139/80-89 mmHg. Stage 2 is 140/90 mmHg or higher. Hypertensive crisis is above 180/120 mmHg and requires immediate medical attention. Risk factors include obesity, smoking, excessive alcohol, high sodium diet, lack of exercise, stress, and family history. Treatment includes lifestyle changes (DASH diet, sodium reduction, exercise, weight loss), ACE inhibitors, ARBs, beta-blockers, calcium channel blockers, and diuretics. Untreated hypertension can cause heart attack, stroke, kidney failure, and vision loss.

# Asthma
Asthma is a chronic respiratory condition causing airway inflammation and narrowing. Symptoms include wheezing, shortness of breath, chest tightness, and coughing (especially at night or early morning). Triggers include allergens (dust mites, pollen, pet dander), exercise, cold air, smoke, stress, and respiratory infections. Diagnosis is confirmed by spirometry showing improved airflow after bronchodilator use. Treatment includes quick-relief inhalers (albuterol), daily controller medications (inhaled corticosteroids), leukotriene modifiers, and biologics for severe cases. Avoiding triggers and having an asthma action plan are essential.

# Coronary Artery Disease
Coronary artery disease (CAD) occurs when plaque (cholesterol and other substances) builds up in heart arteries, reducing blood flow to the heart muscle. Symptoms include chest pain (angina), shortness of breath, and heart attacks. Risk factors include smoking, high cholesterol, diabetes, hypertension, obesity, physical inactivity, and family history. Diagnosis includes stress tests, echocardiograms, and coronary angiography. Treatment includes statins, aspirin, beta-blockers, ACE inhibitors, angioplasty with stenting, and coronary artery bypass surgery. Lifestyle changes include a heart-healthy diet, regular exercise, smoking cessation, and stress management.

# COPD
Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) includes emphysema (destruction of air sacs) and chronic bronchitis (inflammation of bronchial tubes). It causes progressive, irreversible breathing difficulties. Smoking is the primary cause (85-90% of cases). Symptoms include chronic cough with mucus, wheezing, shortness of breath, chest tightness, and frequent respiratory infections. Diagnosis is confirmed by spirometry showing reduced FEV1/FVC ratio. Treatment includes bronchodilators, inhaled corticosteroids, oxygen therapy, pulmonary rehabilitation, and smoking cessation. In advanced stages, lung volume reduction surgery or lung transplantation may be considered.

# Alzheimer's Disease
Alzheimer's is a progressive, irreversible brain disorder causing memory loss and cognitive decline. It accounts for 60-80% of dementia cases. Early signs include forgetting recent events, confusion with time or place, difficulty with familiar tasks, mood and personality changes, and poor judgment. As it progresses, patients lose the ability to communicate, recognize family, and perform basic self-care. There is no cure, but medications like donepezil, memantine, and aducanumab can temporarily slow symptoms. Prevention includes mental stimulation, physical exercise, social engagement, Mediterranean diet, and managing cardiovascular risk factors.

# Influenza
The flu (influenza) is a viral respiratory infection caused by influenza viruses. Symptoms include sudden onset of fever, cough, sore throat, runny or stuffy nose, muscle or body aches, headaches, and fatigue. Some people experience vomiting and diarrhea, especially children. Annual vaccination is the best prevention, ideally administered before flu season (October). Treatment includes rest, fluids, acetaminophen or ibuprofen for fever, and antiviral medications like oseltamivir (Tamiflu) if started within 48 hours of symptom onset. High-risk groups (elderly, pregnant women, young children, immunocompromised) should seek early medical attention.

# Common Cold
The common cold is a viral infection of the nose and throat, most commonly caused by rhinoviruses. Symptoms include runny or stuffy nose, sore throat, cough, mild headache, slight fatigue, and mild congestion. Symptoms typically appear 1-3 days after exposure and last 7-10 days. There is no cure; treatment focuses on symptom relief with rest, fluids, throat lozenges, decongestants, and over-the-counter pain relievers. Prevention includes frequent handwashing, avoiding close contact with sick people, and not touching the face with unwashed hands.

# Pneumonia
Pneumonia is an infection inflaming the air sacs (alveoli) in one or both lungs, which may fill with fluid or pus. Symptoms include cough with phlegm or pus, fever, chills, shortness of breath, sharp chest pain that worsens with breathing or coughing, fatigue, and confusion in older adults. It can be bacterial (most common, treated with antibiotics), viral (antiviral medications or supportive care), fungal (antifungal medications), or aspiration-related. Diagnosis includes chest X-ray, blood tests, and sputum culture. Prevention includes pneumococcal vaccine, flu vaccine, handwashing, and not smoking.

# Depression
Depression (major depressive disorder) is a mood disorder causing persistent sadness, loss of interest, and reduced energy lasting at least two weeks. Symptoms include fatigue, sleep changes (insomnia or oversleeping), appetite changes, difficulty concentrating, feelings of worthlessness, and thoughts of death or suicide. It affects approximately 280 million people worldwide. Treatment includes psychotherapy (CBT, interpersonal therapy), antidepressants (SSRIs, SNRIs), lifestyle changes (exercise, sleep hygiene, social support), and in severe cases, electroconvulsive therapy (ECT) or transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS).

# Urine Test (Urinalysis)
A urinalysis is a comprehensive examination of urine that includes visual inspection, chemical dipstick testing, and microscopic analysis. It is used to detect and manage conditions such as urinary tract infections (UTIs), kidney disease, diabetes, and liver disease. Normal urine values: Color is nearly colorless to dark yellow. Specific gravity is 1.005-1.030 (measures urine concentration). pH is 4.6-8.0 (average 6.0). Glucose should be absent; presence indicates diabetes or kidney disease. Ketones should be absent; presence indicates uncontrolled diabetes, starvation, or ketogenic diet. Protein should be absent or trace only; 1+ is approximately 30 mg/dL, 2+ is 100 mg/dL, 3+ is 300 mg/dL, 4+ is 1000 mg/dL — elevated protein may indicate kidney damage, infection, or preeclampsia. Bilirubin should be absent; presence indicates liver disease or bile duct obstruction. Blood should be absent; presence (hematuria) may indicate infection, kidney stones, trauma, or cancer. Nitrite should be negative; positive indicates bacterial infection (especially E. coli). Leukocyte esterase should be negative; positive indicates white blood cells and possible UTI. Urobilinogen normal range is 0.1-1.0 mg/dL; elevated levels indicate liver damage. Microscopic examination: Red blood cells (RBC) normal is 0-2 per high power field (hpf); elevated RBC (hematuria) may indicate infection, kidney stones, glomerulonephritis, or cancer. White blood cells (WBC) normal is 0-5 per hpf; elevated WBC (pyuria) indicates UTI or kidney infection. Bacteria should be absent; presence may indicate UTI. Casts should be absent; their presence indicates kidney disease. Crystals may be normal in small amounts; excessive crystals may indicate kidney stones.

# Urinary Tract Infection (UTI)
A urinary tract infection (UTI) is an infection in any part of the urinary system, most commonly the bladder (cystitis) or urethra (urethritis). Women are at higher risk due to shorter urethra. Symptoms include burning sensation during urination, frequent urge to urinate, passing small amounts of urine, cloudy or strong-smelling urine, blood in urine (hematuria), pelvic pain in women, and rectal pain in men. Kidney infection (pyelonephritis) symptoms include fever, chills, flank pain, nausea, and vomiting. Diagnosis is confirmed by urinalysis showing positive leukocyte esterase, positive nitrite, elevated WBC (>5/hpf), and bacteria presence, followed by urine culture to identify the specific bacteria. Treatment includes antibiotics (trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole, nitrofurantoin, or ciprofloxacin) for 3-7 days. Prevention includes drinking plenty of water, urinating after intercourse, wiping front to back, and avoiding irritating feminine products. Cranberry products may help prevent recurrent UTIs in some women.

# Complete Blood Count (CBC)
A complete blood count (CBC) is the most common blood test, measuring three main cell types: red blood cells, white blood cells, and platelets. Normal reference ranges for adults: White blood cells (WBC) — 4,000-10,000 cells per microliter (mcL); elevated WBC indicates infection, inflammation, or leukemia; low WBC may indicate bone marrow problems or viral infection. Red blood cells (RBC) — males: 4.5-6.1 million/mcL, females: 4.0-5.4 million/mcL; low RBC indicates anemia; high RBC may indicate dehydration or lung disease. Hemoglobin (Hb) — males: 13-17 g/dL, females: 11.5-15.5 g/dL; low hemoglobin indicates anemia (iron deficiency, vitamin B12 deficiency, chronic disease); high hemoglobin may indicate dehydration or polycythemia. Hematocrit (Hct) — males: 40-55%, females: 36-48%; measures the percentage of blood volume made up by red blood cells. Mean corpuscular volume (MCV) — 80-100 fL; measures average red blood cell size; low MCV indicates microcytic anemia (iron deficiency); high MCV indicates macrocytic anemia (B12 or folate deficiency). Mean corpuscular hemoglobin (MCH) — 27-31 picograms per cell. Mean corpuscular hemoglobin concentration (MCHC) — 32-36 g/dL. Platelet count — 150,000-400,000 cells/mcL; low platelets (thrombocytopenia) increase bleeding risk; high platelets (thrombocytosis) may indicate inflammation or bone marrow disorder. WBC differential: Neutrophils — 2,500-7,000/mcL (40-70% of WBCs), elevated in bacterial infections; Lymphocytes — 1,000-4,800/mcL (20-40% of WBCs), elevated in viral infections; Monocytes — 200-800/mcL (2-8% of WBCs); Eosinophils — less than 500/mcL (1-4% of WBCs), elevated in allergies or parasitic infections; Basophils — less than 300/mcL (0.5-1% of WBCs).

# Kidney Function Tests
Kidney function tests evaluate how well the kidneys are filtering waste from the blood. Creatinine is a waste product from muscle metabolism; normal serum creatinine is 0.6-1.2 mg/dL (females: 0.6-1.1 mg/dL, males: 0.7-1.3 mg/dL). Elevated creatinine indicates impaired kidney function, dehydration, muscle injury, or certain medications. Blood urea nitrogen (BUN) measures urea nitrogen in blood; normal is 7-20 mg/dL. Elevated BUN indicates kidney disease, dehydration, high protein diet, or gastrointestinal bleeding. The BUN-to-creatinine ratio normal is 10:1 to 20:1; a ratio above 20:1 suggests dehydration or gastrointestinal bleeding; below 10:1 suggests liver disease. Estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) calculates how much blood the kidneys filter per minute; normal is above 90 mL/min/1.73m². eGFR below 60 for three months indicates chronic kidney disease (CKD). Stages of CKD: Stage 1 (eGFR 90+ with kidney damage), Stage 2 (eGFR 60-89), Stage 3a (45-59), Stage 3b (30-44), Stage 4 (15-29), Stage 5 (below 15, kidney failure requiring dialysis or transplant). Electrolytes are also assessed: Sodium (Na+) normal 136-145 mEq/L, Potassium (K+) normal 3.5-5.0 mEq/L (elevated potassium can cause dangerous heart rhythms), Chloride (Cl-) normal 98-106 mEq/L, Bicarbonate (HCO3-) normal 22-28 mEq/L. Calcium normal is 8.5-10.5 mg/dL, Phosphorus normal is 2.5-4.5 mg/dL.

# HIV and AIDS
HIV (Human Immunodeficiency Virus) attacks the body's immune system, specifically CD4 cells (T cells). Without treatment, HIV progressively destroys the immune system, leading to AIDS (Acquired Immunodeficiency Syndrome). HIV transmission occurs through unprotected sexual contact, sharing needles, mother-to-child during pregnancy/birth/breastfeeding, and blood transfusions (rare in developed countries now). HIV does NOT spread through casual contact, air, water, insects, or sharing utensils. Acute HIV infection (2-4 weeks after exposure) symptoms include fever, chills, rash, night sweats, muscle aches, sore throat, fatigue, swollen lymph nodes, and mouth ulcers — similar to flu. Clinical latency stage (asymptomatic period) can last 5-10 years without treatment; the virus is still active but reproduces at low levels. AIDS is the most advanced stage of HIV infection, diagnosed when CD4 count drops below 200 cells/microL (normal: 500-1,600 cells/microL) or when certain opportunistic infections develop. CD4 count stages: Normal is 500-1,600 cells/microL; Stage 1 HIV is 200-500 cells/microL; Stage 3 (AIDS) is below 200 cells/microL. Viral load measures the amount of HIV RNA in blood; undetectable viral load (below 200 copies/mL) means the person cannot transmit HIV through sex (U=U: Undetectable = Untransmittable). Treatment: Antiretroviral therapy (ART) is the gold standard, typically a combination of 3 drugs from 2 or more classes (NRTIs, NNRTIs, protease inhibitors, integrase inhibitors). ART should be started as soon as possible after diagnosis. With proper treatment, people with HIV can live near-normal lifespans. Prevention includes PrEP (pre-exposure prophylaxis) for high-risk individuals, PEP (post-exposure prophylaxis) within 72 hours of potential exposure, condoms, needle exchange programs, and mother-to-child prevention during pregnancy.

# Sexually Transmitted Infections (STIs/STDs)
Sexually transmitted infections are infections spread through sexual contact. Chlamydia is the most common bacterial STI, caused by Chlamydia trachomatis. Many people have no symptoms. When symptoms occur: burning during urination, unusual discharge, lower abdominal pain, and bleeding between periods in women. Untreated chlamydia can cause pelvic inflammatory disease (PID), infertility, and ectopic pregnancy. Treatment: antibiotics (azithromycin or doxycycline). Gonorrhea is caused by Neisseria gonorrhoeae. Symptoms include painful urination, increased urinary frequency, unusual vaginal or penile discharge, and in women, abnormal vaginal bleeding. Treatment: dual antibiotic therapy (ceftriaxone injection plus azithromycin). Syphilis is caused by Treponema pallidum and progresses through stages: Primary — painless sore (chancre) at infection site; Secondary — rash on palms and soles, fever, swollen lymph nodes; Latent — no symptoms for years; Tertiary — severe damage to heart, brain, and other organs. Treatment: penicillin injections. Genital herpes is caused by herpes simplex virus (HSV-1 or HSV-2). Symptoms include painful blisters or sores on genitals, itching, burning during urination, and flu-like symptoms during first outbreak. Outbreaks may recur periodically. There is no cure; antiviral medications (acyclovir, valacyclovir) reduce outbreak frequency and severity. HPV (Human Papillomavirus) is the most common STI; most sexually active people get it at some point. Most strains clear on their own, but high-risk strains can cause cervical, anal, throat, and other cancers. Low-risk strains cause genital warts. Prevention: HPV vaccine (Gardasil 9) recommended for ages 9-26. Hepatitis B is a viral liver infection spread through blood and bodily fluids. Can become chronic. Prevention: Hepatitis B vaccine. Trichomoniasis is a parasitic STI causing itching, burning, discharge, and painful urination. Treatment: metronidazole or tinidazole. General prevention: consistent condom use, regular STI screening, mutual monogamy, vaccination (HPV, Hepatitis B), and open communication with partners.

# Anemia
Anemia is a condition in which the body lacks enough healthy red blood cells or hemoglobin to carry adequate oxygen to tissues. Symptoms include fatigue, weakness, pale skin, shortness of breath, dizziness, cold hands and feet, and chest pain. Types: Iron deficiency anemia is the most common type worldwide; caused by blood loss, poor diet, pregnancy, or malabsorption. Diagnosis: low hemoglobin, low MCV (microcytic), low ferritin. Treatment: iron supplements, dietary iron (red meat, spinach, fortified cereals). Vitamin B12 deficiency anemia (pernicious anemia) is caused by lack of intrinsic factor or poor B12 intake (common in vegans). Diagnosis: high MCV (macrocytic), low B12 levels. Treatment: B12 injections or high-dose oral supplements. Folate deficiency anemia is caused by poor diet, alcoholism, or certain medications. Treatment: folic acid supplements. Hemolytic anemia occurs when red blood cells are destroyed faster than they can be made. Sickle cell anemia is a genetic disorder causing misshapen red blood cells. Aplastic anemia is caused by bone marrow failure. Diagnosis includes CBC, reticulocyte count, iron studies, B12 and folate levels, and sometimes bone marrow biopsy.

# Liver Function Tests
Liver function tests evaluate liver health and detect liver disease. Alanine aminotransferase (ALT) normal is 7-56 U/L; elevated in hepatitis, liver damage, or medication toxicity. Aspartate aminotransferase (AST) normal is 10-40 U/L; elevated in liver disease, heart attack, or muscle damage. Alkaline phosphatase (ALP) normal is 44-147 U/L; elevated in bile duct obstruction, liver disease, or bone disorders. Total bilirubin normal is 0.3-1.2 mg/dL; elevated in jaundice, liver disease, or hemolytic anemia. Direct bilirubin normal is 0.0-0.3 mg/dL. Total protein normal is 6.0-8.3 g/dL. Albumin normal is 3.5-5.0 g/dL; low albumin indicates liver disease or malnutrition. Gamma-glutamyl transferase (GGT) normal is 9-48 U/L; elevated in alcohol abuse or bile duct disease. Common liver conditions: Hepatitis A (viral, food/water-borne, usually acute, vaccine available), Hepatitis B (viral, blood/body fluids, can be chronic, vaccine available), Hepatitis C (viral, blood-borne, often chronic, curable with direct-acting antivirals), Cirrhosis (permanent scarring of liver tissue, often from chronic alcohol abuse or chronic hepatitis), Fatty liver disease (fat accumulation in liver, associated with obesity and diabetes).

# Thyroid Function Tests
Thyroid function tests evaluate the thyroid gland, which regulates metabolism. Thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH) normal is 0.4-4.0 mIU/L. Free T4 normal is 0.8-1.8 ng/dL. Free T3 normal is 2.3-4.2 pg/mL. Hypothyroidism (underactive thyroid) is characterized by high TSH and low free T4. Symptoms include fatigue, weight gain, cold intolerance, constipation, dry skin, hair loss, depression, and memory problems. Most common cause is Hashimoto's thyroiditis (autoimmune). Treatment: levothyroxine (synthetic T4). Hyperthyroidism (overactive thyroid) is characterized by low TSH and high free T4/T3. Symptoms include weight loss, rapid heartbeat, anxiety, tremors, heat intolerance, increased sweating, and frequent bowel movements. Most common cause is Graves' disease (autoimmune). Treatment: antithyroid medications (methimazole), radioactive iodine, or surgery.

# Electrolyte Imbalance
Electrolytes are minerals in the blood that carry an electric charge and are essential for normal body function. Sodium (Na+) normal is 136-145 mEq/L. Hyponatremia (low sodium, below 136 mEq/L) can cause confusion, seizures, and coma; causes include excessive water intake, heart failure, kidney disease, and certain medications. Hypernatremia (high sodium, above 145 mEq/L) causes thirst, weakness, and confusion; usually due to dehydration. Potassium (K+) normal is 3.5-5.0 mEq/L. Hypokalemia (low potassium) causes muscle weakness, cramps, and abnormal heart rhythms; causes include diuretics, vomiting, and diarrhea. Hyperkalemia (high potassium) can cause dangerous heart arrhythmias; causes include kidney failure, certain medications (ACE inhibitors, potassium-sparing diuretics), and tissue damage. Calcium (Ca2+) normal is 8.5-10.5 mg/dL. Hypocalcemia causes muscle spasms, tingling, and seizures. Hypercalcemia causes kidney stones, bone pain, and confusion. Magnesium (Mg2+) normal is 1.5-2.0 mg/dL. Low magnesium causes muscle cramps, tremors, and arrhythmias.

# Kidney Stones
Kidney stones are hard deposits of minerals and salts that form inside the kidneys. Symptoms include severe pain in the back, side, or groin (renal colic), blood in urine, nausea and vomiting, frequent urge to urinate, and cloudy or foul-smelling urine. Types: Calcium stones (most common, 80%), Uric acid stones (associated with gout, high-purine diet), Struvite stones (associated with UTIs), Cystine stones (rare, genetic). Diagnosis includes CT scan, ultrasound, and urine analysis showing crystals and blood. Treatment for small stones (<5mm): hydration (2-3 liters of water daily), pain management, and alpha-blockers (tamsulosin) to help passage. Large stones may require extracorporeal shock wave lithotripsy (ESWL), ureteroscopy, or percutaneous nephrolithotomy. Prevention includes drinking plenty of water, reducing sodium intake, limiting animal protein, and for calcium stones, normal dietary calcium (not restricted) with reduced oxalate foods (spinach, rhubarb, nuts).

# Gastroesophageal Reflux Disease (GERD)
GERD is a chronic digestive disorder where stomach acid frequently flows back into the esophagus. Symptoms include heartburn (burning chest pain), regurgitation of food or sour liquid, difficulty swallowing, chronic cough, hoarseness, and asthma-like symptoms. Complications include esophagitis, esophageal stricture, Barrett's esophagus (pre-cancerous changes), and esophageal cancer. Treatment includes lifestyle modifications (weight loss, elevating head of bed, avoiding late meals, reducing trigger foods like spicy/fatty foods, caffeine, alcohol), antacids, H2 blockers (famotidine), and proton pump inhibitors (omeprazole, pantoprazole).

# Chronic Kidney Disease (CKD)
Chronic kidney disease is the gradual loss of kidney function over time. Stages are based on eGFR: Stage 1 (eGFR 90+ with kidney damage), Stage 2 (60-89, mild), Stage 3a (45-59, mild to moderate), Stage 3b (30-44, moderate to severe), Stage 4 (15-29, severe), Stage 5 (below 15, kidney failure). Symptoms in early stages are often absent. Later symptoms include fatigue, swelling in ankles and feet, shortness of breath, nausea, loss of appetite, metallic taste in mouth, and confusion. Causes include diabetes (most common), hypertension, glomerulonephritis, polycystic kidney disease, and prolonged urinary tract obstruction. Management includes controlling blood pressure and blood sugar, diet modifications (low sodium, low protein, potassium and phosphorus restrictions in advanced stages), medications, and in end-stage renal disease, dialysis or kidney transplantation.

# level
Real business-cycle theory (RBC theory) is a class of new classical macroeconomics models in which business-cycle fluctuations are accounted for by real, in contrast to nominal, shocks. RBC theory sees business cycle fluctuations as the efficient response to exogenous changes in the real economic environment. That is, the level of national output necessarily maximizes expected utility.
In RBC models, business cycles are described as "real" because they reflect optimal adjustments by economic agents rather than failures of markets to clear. As a result, RBC theory suggests that governments should concentrate on long-term structural change rather than intervention through discretionary fiscal or monetary policy. These ideas are strongly associated with freshwater economics within the neoclassical economics tradition, particularly the Chicago School of Economics.

# images
Kidney cancer, also known as renal cancer, is a group of cancers that start in the kidney. Symptoms may include blood in the urine, a lump in the abdomen, or back pain. Fever, weight loss, and tiredness may also occur. Complications can include spread to the lungs or brain.
The main types of kidney cancer are renal cell cancer (RCC), transitional cell cancer (TCC), and Wilms' tumor. RCC makes up about 80% of kidney cancers, and TCC accounts for most of the rest. Risk factors for RCC and TCC include smoking, certain pain medications, previous bladder cancer, being overweight, high blood pressure, certain chemicals, and a family history. Risk factors for Wilms' tumor include a family history and certain genetic disorders such as WAGR syndrome. Diagnosis may be suspected based on symptoms, urine testing, and medical imaging. It is confirmed by tissue biopsy.

In sports, spectators including sports fandom and sportswriters, as well as participants themselves have discussed players, coaches, teams, and related personalities in regards to being the greatest in their field, or sometimes across sports.
